ETS2: do we know what we want to measure and achieve?

Energy
Public policy
ETS2 revenue should go mainly into our own energy sources. And success needs to be measured so that the indicators show what we actually care about.
Published

29 October 2026

From 2028 the European Union will put a price on CO₂ emissions from heating buildings and from motor fuels. Allowances in the new emissions trading system, ETS2, will be bought not by households but by fuel suppliers1. The costs will, however, feed through to prices: by our calculation, the average Czech household will pay €218 a year more for energy in 2032 because of ETS2, just under one per cent of its net income2. This year the EU postponed the start of the system by a year3, and Czechia and Italy are now pushing for it to be suspended4.

If ETS2 does go ahead, the state will earn new revenue from selling allowances, which it must spend on climate and social measures5. Before we decide what to do with that money, we should be clear about two things: what we want to achieve, and how we will know that we have achieved it. The answer to the first question lies in the situation Europe is in. For the second, our research helps: the indicators by which success will be judged can assess the same support in very different ways.

What we want to achieve

Reducing CO₂ emissions is a sensible goal. But Europe pursues it from a weaker starting position than its competitors.

Energy in Europe is expensive, and we import most of it. The 2024 Draghi report on the future of European competitiveness states that EU companies pay two to three times more for electricity than companies in the US, and four to five times more for natural gas. It names Europe’s lack of natural resources as the main cause, but also flaws in the common energy market and high taxes. And it warns that if Europe fails to coordinate its policies, decarbonisation risks running contrary to competitiveness and growth6. In 2024 the EU imported 57% of the energy available to it, and two thirds of these imports were oil and petroleum products7.

Our main competitors do not impose the same costs on themselves. Since January 2026 the United States has not been a party to the Paris Agreement8, and it has declared a “national energy emergency” to expand domestic energy production, including oil and natural gas9. China has an emissions trading system, but for now it does not tell firms how much they may emit in total10.

Regulation comes on top of expensive energy. According to the Draghi report, more than 60% of EU companies see it as an obstacle to investment11. Between 2019 and 2024 the EU passed around 13,000 acts, while around 3,500 laws and 2,000 resolutions were adopted at federal level in the US12. An overall figure for how much regulation adds to the price of European products is missing13. Partial estimates do exist: EU legislation from 2004 to 2014 cost the chemical industry about 2% of its turnover and 12% of its value added, roughly one euro in every eight that the sector itself created14.

Some of the regulation we add ourselves. In October this year the President of the European Commission, Ursula von der Leyen, proposed a “pact against overregulation”: member states should show which obligations come from EU law and which they have added themselves15. Czechia has improved as far as new laws are concerned: under a 2016 government methodology it transposes directives in their minimal form, and unjustified gold-plating has almost disappeared from new legislation16. It persists, however, in older laws, in MPs’ amendments and in practice17. For example, when a building that was built and last substantially altered before 1947 is sold or let, no energy performance certificate is needed if both parties agree in writing18. According to an analysis by the Association for International Affairs (AMO), however, the media and firms that prepare such assessments often keep quiet about exemptions of this kind19. Exemptions that spare people administrative burden are welcome; people must, however, be made aware of them. According to the Czech Chamber of Commerce, administration costs Czech businesses around CZK 72 billion a year20.

So what do we want to achieve? For the EU and Czechia to depend less on imported fossil fuels, which is the right thing to do regardless of the climate, and for products made in Europe not to be made more expensive by unnecessary rules. ETS2 money can contribute to this, but that depends on where it goes and on how its contribution will be judged.

How we will know it is working

The rules for ETS2 money have two parts. Revenue from some of the allowances goes to the EU’s Social Climate Fund, from which Czechia can receive up to CZK 50 billion over 2026 to 203221. The state must add at least a quarter of the costs of its Social Climate Plan22. At most 37.5% of these costs may go to temporary direct income support for households, so most of the money must go to measures with a lasting effect, such as building renovation, clean heating or zero-emission transport23. The state keeps the remaining ETS2 revenue and must use it for climate purposes, giving priority to social impacts24.

With the Fund, measurement decides. The Commission pays out the money only if the state meets the targets of its plan, and these must include a reduction in the number of households in energy poverty25. Energy poverty, however, cannot be measured directly. Statisticians estimate it with indicators that count as energy poor, for example, a household with a low income and at the same time high energy costs. Each indicator defines “high costs” differently, and that is exactly what matters.

We studied how the indicators respond to support for households with a model that carries the effects of ETS2 on prices and incomes across the whole economy through to the budgets of 3,233 households from household surveys. Together they represent all Czech households. Unlike simple estimates, which merely apply higher prices to today’s consumption, households in the model respond to changes in prices and incomes in the way the data on their spending show. The model also tracks their savings and debts, so it captures how a renovation is financed, and for every result we report how precisely the data can measure it26.

We compared what happens when the same €745 million over 2028 to 2032 goes to insulation and heat pumps and, for comparison, to direct payments to households27. The main finding is that what the indicators record depends as much on how they are constructed as on the policy itself28. Three examples show this.

  • A direct payment brings households relief. But they spend part of the money on heating, and an indicator that judges high costs by the level of energy spending reads this as a deterioration. As a result, 87% of the relief disappears from it29. It is rather like the scales for someone who has started exercising: they show more kilograms even though the person is in better shape.
  • Insulation and heat pumps reduce energy poverty, as measured by indicators that combine low income and high costs, by 7.1 to 10.5%. They change the house itself, so their effect lasts: three years after the programme ends, the reduction is still at least 5.4%30.
  • How it is paid for matters too. An indicator that tracks a household’s savings as well as its income and costs records whether the household paid its share of a renovation from savings or with a loan. If it draws on its savings, the indicator counts this as a deterioration: for a heat pump, the fall in savings wipes out 36% of the improvement brought by lower bills31. The same renovation can therefore come out better or worse simply depending on how it was paid for.

The official indicators, meanwhile, move by only fractions of a percentage point under every programme32. Goals and measurement rules therefore need to be set before the money starts to flow, and instead of a single number we should track several indicators, each with its precision33.

Where ETS2 money should go

Support that changes the house itself keeps working after the payments stop. ETS2 money should work the same way across the whole economy: it should reduce dependence on imported fuels for good.

The revenue the state keeps from ETS2 may already be used, among other things, for renewable energy, transmission grids and other technologies that contribute to the transition to a safe and sustainable low-carbon economy34. These provisions do not mention nuclear power explicitly. Revenue from the existing allowance system for industry and power generation, ETS1, has gone mainly to the State Environmental Fund since this year. According to the Ministry of the Environment, it is to fund, for example, new renewable sources, the modernisation of heating plants, energy savings and support for low- and middle-income households35.

I would apply the same logic to ETS2, and more boldly. Most of the money from allowances should strengthen the resilience of the economy as a whole, that is, finance our own energy sources, both nuclear and renewable, and the grids that connect them. The Draghi report, too, counts on both kinds of sources with low running costs36. A smaller part should go to targeted support for the households hit hardest by ETS2. According to our model, these are mainly households heating with gas and pensioners living alone37.

China shows that it is possible to invest in one’s own energy sources on a large scale. In 2025 alone, according to the International Energy Agency, it added almost 500 gigawatts of renewable capacity, more than 60% of global growth38. Such investments are, however, expensive. For the two new units at Dukovany the state will pay the Korean company KHNP CZK 407 billion at current prices39, and in August this year the Minister of Industry, Karel Havlíček, acknowledged that, including the cost of money, they will cost CZK 600 to 700 billion40. Construction is to be covered by a state loan, which the European Commission has been examining in depth since December 202541.

The answer to our opening question

So what should we aim for? In my view, greater self-sufficiency in energy, and production that rules do not make unnecessarily expensive. The way there is a mix of three steps: ease the regulations that make products unnecessarily expensive, in Brussels and at home; direct ETS2 revenue mainly into our own energy sources and grids; and help the households hit hardest by ETS2 in a way that lowers their bills for good. Throughout, the state should serve citizens and businesses, not the other way round: if it needs certain data, it should obtain them itself rather than shifting that obligation onto households and firms.

And how will we know we have succeeded? When price swings on world markets feed through to household bills only very slightly. Such a yardstick tracks directly what we care about, and unlike energy poverty indicators it does not depend on whether a household paid for its renovation from savings or with a loan.
